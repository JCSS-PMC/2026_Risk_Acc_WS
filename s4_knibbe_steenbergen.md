# Including CO₂ Emissions in Risk Acceptance Criteria – Augmented LQI Criterion and Combined Optimisation

## Summary

Jelle Knibbe, PhD candidate at TU Delft in collaboration with TNO and supervised by Raphaël Steenbergen, presented the work online.

**Recap of the conventional LQI criterion.** An increase in safety, quantified by a reduction in mortality $-dm$, has to be balanced by the marginal cost $dC$; with the societal willingness to pay $(G/q)\,C_y$ this gives a net-benefit criterion, which for structures – with the failure probability $P_f(x)$ as a function of the decision variable, $N_f$ fatalities at failure and the parameters of the cost model – becomes the relative life-saving cost $K_1$ used in ISO 2394. Graphically, with a linear cost function, the point where $-dP_f/dx$ meets $K_1$ gives the minimum acceptable $x$ and hence the acceptable $\beta$.

**The environmental gap.** Construction is responsible for about 30 % of global CO₂ emissions (cement alone about 8 %), and emissions affect society as a whole. The conventional criterion covers two of the three pillars of sustainability – social (fatalities) and economic (cost) – but not the environmental one.

**Augmented LQI criterion.** Shadow-costed emissions are added to the safety cost: the emissions are modelled with the same Rackwitz renewal model that underlies the ISO cost function (initial emissions $E_0 + E_1 x$, repeated at obsolescence and at failure, plus emissions $E_H$ at failure), converted with a shadow price $c_e$ and added to the safety cost. The resulting relative life-saving cost $K_{aug}(x)$ has emission terms both above the division line (marginal cost of constructing and reconstructing – a *push* of the acceptable failure probability upwards) and below it (emissions at failure, next to the fatalities – a *pull* downwards). Because the relative contribution to the cost term is larger than to the consequence term, there is a net push: the requirement becomes more lenient when emissions are included.

**Representative application.** A steel beam in bending, adapted from Hingorani and Köhler, with the section modulus as decision variable, stochastic models from the Eurocode background (consistent with the PMC), material cost, emission and shadow-price data from the literature and the fatality model of Hingorani fitted to actual collapses. The conventional criterion gives $\beta_{acc} = 4.30$, the augmented criterion 4.18, i.e. $\Delta\beta = 0.12$ for a shadow cost of 0.80 EUR/kg CO₂-eq – not spectacular, but in the ISO tables about "half a cost class". A variation study shows: higher steel cost lowers both betas (push) and shrinks $\Delta\beta$; more fatalities raise both betas (pull) with $\Delta\beta$ constant unless $N_f$ is very small; a higher shadow price increases $\Delta\beta$. Across literature ranges $\Delta\beta$ lies between 0.1 and 0.3, and for large enough $N_f$ it correlates neatly with the ratio of shadow price to steel cost. This suggests that *climate classes* could be defined for norms – from the shadow price and the material's emission intensity as a dimensionless ratio – indicating a $\Delta\beta$ for a class of structures as an addition to the ISO 2394 tables.

**A multi-objective alternative.** Since shadow costing (putting a price on CO₂) can be controversial, total cost and emissions can be treated as separate objectives. The Pareto front between the cost minimum ("status quo", $\beta_{opt} \approx 4.3$) and the emissions-only optimum ($\beta_{opt} \approx 3.45$, at much higher cost) connects every trade-off to an optimal β. Constraining one objective relative to the status quo (a desired relative emission decrease or an allowed cost increase) and minimising the other gives an "unfolded" Pareto–β front; a visual heuristic – emissions decrease roughly linearly while costs increase faster and faster – suggests that below about $\beta_{opt,aug} \approx 3.8$ ($\Delta\beta \approx 0.5$) the trade-off becomes unfavourable. This is case specific, cannot be generalised like the shadow-cost approach, and leaves open the question of an acceptable lower bound.

**Conclusions and discussion points.** Emissions can be included in safety requirements and lead to slightly more lenient requirements; shadow costing is the more generalisable route ($\Delta\beta$ = 0.1–0.3), the two-objective optimisation suits a specific structure. Open questions: is a *net pull* possible (e.g. a bridge whose closure after failure causes detour emissions)? And should the material choice – through its emission intensity – influence the safety requirement?

## Discussion

*Summary of the discussion following the presentation (edited from the recording).*

Colin Caprani asked how the trivial solution of the emission optimisation – not building at all, the first advice in UK guidance – was avoided. The speakers explained that building is taken as given, and that within the renewal model zero material implies a very large failure probability and continuous reconstruction, which is why the Pareto front does not end at zero emissions; the infinite renewal model of Rackwitz is a rough assumption for an individual structure, and a case with an existing bridge, in which doing nothing is an option and strengthening also causes emissions, is in preparation – which, as Jannie Nielsen remarked, provides the base case discussed the day before. Nielsen also asked about the ordering of the lines for different *N<sub>f</sub>* in the Δβ plot; the neat relation only emerges for sufficiently large *N<sub>f</sub>*.

Arnold Yuan found the direction of the result counter-intuitive: considering emissions relaxes the safety requirement, which he read as designing for a shorter life, whereas in the renewal model a safer structure is reconstructed less often. This led to the discount rate on emissions. The example uses 3 % for everything, as in ISO 2394; differential discounting with a lower rate for environmental effects is work in progress, in line with the literature. Jochen Köhler questioned whether discounting applies to emissions at all – are 100 units of emissions in the future fewer units today? – a question the speakers found very difficult; Arnold Yuan pointed to the warming potential and decay rates of the different greenhouse gases as the relevant climate-science concept. Jochen Köhler closed by noting that the online presentation had worked without any problem.
