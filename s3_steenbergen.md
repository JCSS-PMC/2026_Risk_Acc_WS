# Risk Acceptance in the Netherlands – Flood Defence

**Raphaël Steenbergen, TNO / Ghent University**

Session 3 · Thursday 22 October 2026, 09.47–10.22 (presentation and discussion)

[Presentation slides (PDF, handout)](slides/Steenbergen_Flood_Defences_Netherlands.pdf) – *How safe is safe enough: flood defences in the Netherlands*

## Summary

At Jochen Köhler's request the presentation gave an insight into how the Netherlands deals with its flood defences: how safe is safe enough, how is that established, and how are individual risk, societal risk and economic consequences looked at. The basics were presented; a lot of science and many methods lie behind them.

**Setting.** A map of the maximum water depth in case of flooding shows that large parts of the Netherlands, a delta below sea level, would flood easily without protection – from the sea (storm-surge barriers and dikes) and from the big European rivers. For the flood risk assessment the country is divided into about one hundred *dike rings*, each an area protected by the dike around it; they differ strongly in population and economic value (the Hague and the western Netherlands in dike ring 14, other rings mainly farmland).

**The legal standard.** For each dike ring the Water Act prescribes a maximum probability of inundation. It is derived from two assessments, and the envelope of the two is taken: a *cost optimisation* and an *individual risk* criterion. For the most densely populated areas the societal risk is a third point of attention.

*Cost optimisation.* All costs are brought together in one optimisation: the cost of strengthening the dike, the direct and indirect failure costs (economic models, maps of the economic value per location – cities, harbours, power plants, buildings and economic activities – projected to the economic situation in 2050, since e.g. the value of power plants increases as society becomes more dependent on electricity), and the value of a statistical life, for which 2.7 million euro per person was used. This is a population-average assessment. The optimum differs between dike rings: the most valuable region has a requirement of about 1 in 60 000 years, others about 1 in 3 000 years.

*Individual risk.* Within a dike ring the average may be acceptable while individuals differ. The Water Act therefore gives every citizen a basic protection level: a (local) individual risk of 10<sup>−5</sup> per year, taken for every location in the country, also where no one lives yet. It is calculated as the annual flooding probability at location x times the mortality given flooding for a person who remains exposed there – from simulations of water depth, flow velocity and rate of rise at each location – times the probability of not being evacuated in time; all three factors are quantified.

*Societal risk.* For some dike rings the government recognised that there can be too much societal disruption – critical facilities such as the port of Rotterdam, or a very large number of people affected. F–N curves are used in an informative, not a quantitative way, together with other input, and minor adjustments are made at some locations to prevent social disruption.

**How the probability is calculated.** The community is used to failure probabilities at element level; here it is the probability of inundation of the whole system. A dike ring has many dike sections and hydraulic structures; within a section the geotechnical parameters vary (sub-sections); each section has several failure mechanisms – overtopping, macro-instability, piping, wave attack – which are correlated through the water level. A system reliability analysis with correlation modelling, length effects, combination of mechanisms and of loads with different time scales (fast storm surges, long river discharges) is needed; element failure probabilities are not enough, and dedicated tools (e.g. Hydra-Ring, with Deltares) have been developed. The consequences are modelled on the system as well.

**Absolute or comparative probabilities?** The JCSS community often holds that probabilities are only comparative measures and that the absolute value and its exact interpretation are not important. For this application the speaker does not think so: here failure probabilities are applied to optimise expected utility and to meet the individual risk target, so they have a more absolute character. This requires extra effort: the state of information used has to be verified – does the sub-population chosen for the probabilistic assessment correspond to the degree of information used in the calculation? The failure probability remains a Bayesian estimate reflecting the degree of belief, but one should do the utmost to close the gap with observed failure frequencies: calibration of probabilistic models with local measurements (actual geotechnical parameters, actual water-level distributions), and the use of proven strength, with the state of information kept adequate and not too general.

**Summary.** Cost optimisation, individual risk and societal risk; accurate consequence modelling of financial, economic and human-safety consequences; and a system reliability problem that needs tools, methods and information levels that adequately describe the system.

## Discussion

*Summary of the discussion following the presentation (edited from the recording).*

Jochen Köhler asked whether the dike-ring-specific optimisation is visible in the landscape – neighbouring dike rings with different protection levels – which the speaker confirmed: the requirement ranges from about once in 60 000 years for the economically most valuable region to once in 3 000 years elsewhere. On the absolute character of the probabilities, Köhler noted that the cost optimisation remains a relative comparison, while the individual-risk limit of 10<sup>−5</sup> is the quantity sensitive to the modelling assumptions; the speaker agreed, adding that the flat optimum of the cost optimisation provides some protection there.

Arnold Yuan asked whether the publicly available flood-risk maps show in property prices or insurance premiums; they do not, which prompted a discussion of public perception. The speaker stressed that the maps have to be communicated carefully – everybody enjoys the same basic protection level and is safe enough everywhere, the extra protection following from the economic value of the region – and that the 10<sup>−5</sup> is the same value used for other hazards, such as the induced earthquakes in the north. Jannie Nielsen, Arnold Yuan (basement-flooding maps in Toronto) and Nadieh Meinen (in the chat: the valuable areas are also the expensive ones, which is why the consequences are higher) added that location preferences and prices balance many effects.

André Beck (online) returned to the accuracy of the calculated failure probabilities and asked whether one should not acknowledge from the outset that they cannot be very accurate, given the epistemic uncertainties – and what happens in a lawsuit when an independent engineer disputes them. The speaker's position: a failure probability is neither right nor wrong; it reflects a state of information, which should be as complete as reasonably obtainable, with the gap to observed failure frequencies closed as far as possible. Jochen Köhler added that the focus should be on the assumptions rather than on the result: all assumptions are stated and can be evaluated afterwards for their adequacy given the information available at the time, while the technical execution of the analysis can be judged right or wrong. The speaker stressed that in Dutch practice no individual engineer is decisive: methods are set by an expertise network, results are assessed by the water boards and independently checked by the inspectorate before they reach the minister – the societal value is too large to let one person decide. Mahesh Pandey summarised this as assumptions and models representing a consensus of the engineering knowledge.

Iris Tien (online) asked how non-stationarity is handled. Climate-dependent river discharges and sea level are introduced through the KNMI climate scenarios for each dike section, with 2050 as the horizon, when a new round of assessment and strengthening is foreseen; dikes can be raised repeatedly, whereas the large hydraulic structures are designed for longer horizons with other scenarios. An online participant (presumably Farid Vafadar, following his question in the chat) asked how environmental impacts are considered: not explicitly in the risk optimisation, but as a driver in the choice of strengthening measures and of the operating mode of the storm-surge barriers. Mahesh Pandey closed the session by handing out spare copies of the LQI "blue books" and "black book".
