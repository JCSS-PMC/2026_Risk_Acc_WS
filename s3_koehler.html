Risk Acceptance Regulation in Europe – One Market, Many Rationales#

Jochen Köhler, Norwegian University of Science and Technology (NTNU), Trondheim – JCSS

Session 3 · Thursday 22 October 2026, 09.12–09.45 (presentation and discussion)

Presentation slides (PDF)

Welcome address on behalf of the University of Waterloo#

The second day opened with a welcome address on behalf of the Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering. The speaker introduced the region – close to the Great Lakes, Niagara Falls and Toronto, famous for the largest Oktoberfest outside Germany, home to a high-tech cluster (“the Silicon Valley of Canada”) next to a large Mennonite population – and the University of Waterloo: founded in 1957, 41 000 students, the world’s largest co-operative education programme (engineering students graduate after five years with about two years of work experience), 15 engineering programmes and more than 10 000 engineering students. Waterloo has a rich heritage in risk and reliability research – Niels Lind and Mahesh Pandey – which is part of the reason the workshop was held here. The speaker also recalled his own first contact with the JCSS, a workshop in Denmark in 2004, and said that as a user of the JCSS Probabilistic Model Code in code calibration he was very pleased to see the workshop at Waterloo.

Summary#

The session was intended to give an overview of what exists in terms of risk acceptance in regulations; the first presentation covered Europe, to be followed by the Netherlands and Australia. A full treatment of all technical areas would take two hours; the overview was boiled down to the essentials.

Starting point. There is no European risk acceptance criterion. EU law sets requirements per domain – products, railways, tunnels, major hazards, the built environment – and the safety level is set nationally, in three layers: EU directives and regulations, national law and regulators, and standards and the operators’ own criteria. How safe is safe enough? is answered in every domain, but rarely in the same currency: expected utility, expected cost, number of fatalities – and fatalities themselves can be expressed in several ways (per year, per 50 years, per operating hour, per vehicle-kilometre), as the JCSS meeting would discuss further in connection with individual risk.

What is actually regulated. Three forms of criterion occur: absolute criteria (individual risk, F–N limits, failure frequencies, target reliability β) are clear to communicate but demanding to verify, not least because of epistemic uncertainty; relative criteria (ALARP with a cost test, marginal life-saving cost, “at least as good as” a reference) are more robust and less sensitive to the modelling universe at hand – all relative criteria encountered are consistent with the ALARP philosophy; and implicit criteria, where no risk level is spelled out but the fulfilment of prescriptive rules, codes of practice or consequence classes stands for acceptance. The criteria are set by EU law, national regulators, standards (CEN, ISO, national) or by the operator, audited by the regulator. The old Whitman (1984) picture of annual failure probability against consequences across industries is still shown after forty years; what has changed is that most domains now have a written rationale – but not the same one.

Building structures. EN 1990:2023 gives target reliabilities per consequence class (β = 4.3/3.8/3.3 for 50 years for CC3/CC2/CC1; CC0 and CC4 such as nuclear plants and dams are outside the table and “supplemented by other provisions”). β = 3.8 is the central reference target inherited from EN 1990:2002, derived more than thirty years ago from early considerations with normal distributions, and it sticks. Read precisely, the Eurocode offers the target reliabilities only as a second option; the first option is the partial factor method for general design situations, so one can argue that the Eurocode’s risk acceptance criterion is in fact implicit – design solutions according to the partial factor concept. Life safety and the cost of safety are not explicit. ISO 2394:2015 and the JCSS Probabilistic Model Code distinguish three levels – risk-informed decision making (marginal life-saving cost principle), reliability-based design (optimal and acceptable reliability index, calibrated for generalised situations) and semi-probabilistic design – and Annex G of ISO 2394 introduces the LQI and the SWTP explicitly; it is the only code document valid in Europe that states its life-safety rationale. The “mixed approach” discussed the day before – βopt from cost optimisation with βacc from the SWTP as a lower bound – is the current ISO/JCSS paradigm. The target depends on the consequences but also on the uncertainty structure: with large uncertainties the decision-theoretical argument is for a somewhat lower reliability target. A reliability index carries information only together with the model universe by which it is defined (Ditlevsen 1997; ISO 2394, 8.4); the target is a calibration result, representative of accepted practice within the models, data and conventions of the code, and comparing βt with 10−4 per year in another domain compares two universes. This is also why a common probabilistic model code is needed.

Other domains. Road tunnels: Directive 2004/54/EC requires a risk analysis “where necessary”, by a body independent of the tunnel manager, with a method defined at national level and no criterion. Switzerland (ASTRA 19004/89005) has two thresholds in fatalities per 109 vehicle-km and judges measures in between by the marginal cost per saved life – 6.9 million CHF, with risk aversion explicitly excluded and F–N only for dangerous goods; this traces back to the work of Michael Faber’s group with ASTRA in the 2000s, so the ISO/JCSS concepts have been implemented. Germany (RABT) and Austria (RVS 09.03.11) compare with a reference tunnel that complies with the rules. Offshore, Norway: the operator sets its own acceptance criteria (Management Regulations §9) as upper limits with ALARP (Framework Regulations §11); 10−4 per year since the 1981 NPD guidelines for accidental loads and loss of main safety functions, FAR ≤ 10 for all personnel – unchanged for more than twenty years while the observed FAR fell to about 1. Evaluations for Havtil (Proactima/UiS 2020, Preventor 2021) concluded that quantitative criteria “do not stimulate risk reduction”, analyses become compliance checks and weak criteria are the rational choice for the company – a conclusion the speaker disagrees with – while the 10−4 design criteria keep the solutions sound; options on the table range from a functional regime without criteria to keeping and improving them. Nuclear: absolute probabilistic targets next to deterministic defence in depth; IAEA INSAG-12 core damage frequency below 10−4 per year for existing and 10−5 for new plants; Finland, Switzerland, Sweden and the UK (ONR basic safety level/objective bands, explicitly consistent with HSE’s tolerability framework) differ – and the frequencies are always connected to slightly different events, so comparison is difficult.

Comparison. A table across domains (buildings/EN 1990, ISO 2394/PMC, railways CSM-RA, road tunnels, offshore Norway, nuclear, Dutch flood defence, major hazards, aviation) shows whether life safety is explicit, whether a cost test exists and who sets the criterion. In the building sector there is no cost component at all in the risk acceptance; only consequence classes are distinguished, and the treatment is implicit compared with other businesses – perhaps because there is no existential life-safety crisis in the building sector.

What the speaker takes from this. Absolute limits where the hazard is technical and the public is a third party (aviation, nuclear, Dutch external safety); relative criteria where decisions are project specific and the regulator is mature (UK, CH, NO). The marginal life-saving cost logic is the only rationale shared across domains (ISO 2394 Annex G, Swiss tunnels, UK cost–benefit, Dutch dike optimisation), but the values differ – 6.9 million CHF, £2.2 million, 54.5 million NOK, €4.8 million – and so do the units; agreeing on a common value per country, consistently applied, would already be a big step. Criteria rarely move: β = 3.8 for thirty years, the Norwegian FAR limits for twenty years while the risk fell by a factor of ten – a regulator is criticised from both sides whenever it changes the value, and the direct relation between reliability and material use in the cross-section makes any increase expensive. The Eurocode β is a calibration result, not a societal risk criterion.

For the discussion. Should EN 1990 state its life-safety rationale explicitly, as ISO 2394 does, and who sets the criterion for CC4? One European value per prevented fatality across domains – desirable, or is domain-specific tolerability legitimate? Operator-set (Norway) or regulator-set (UK, NL) criteria – which drives risk reduction? Fixed numbers or relative criteria – how do we keep criteria alive as risks fall?

Discussion#

Summary of the discussion following the presentation (edited from the recording).

Ton Vrouwenvelder (online) found the presentation a little pessimistic about the Eurocode: the consequence-class table has an explicit, if primitive, entry for the loss of human lives, and the class affects the partial factors through KF as well as the robustness and quality-assurance requirements. The speaker conceded the point but noted that the KF factors have never been calibrated, so the reliability level actually reached in CC1 or CC3 is unknown; Vrouwenvelder put the difference at roughly a factor of ten in the fifty-year failure probability. Jannie Nielsen did not consider the missing targets for CC0 and CC4 a problem – nobody analyses a garden shed, and for CC4 national decisions make sense – whereas the speaker argued that precisely for CC4 a framework, such as a reference to the ISO 2394 methods, should be provided.

A participant questioned the figure of 10−4 per year for nuclear core damage as surprisingly high; Arnold Yuan suggested it might be a conditional core damage frequency. A question from the chat (Farid Vafadar) whether the targets refer to members or to the whole building was answered: that is up to the code, the Eurocode being ambivalent and practice mainly member-based.

Arnold Yuan asked how the life-safety rationale relates to performance-based design, where intermediate damage states (inter-storey drift under moderate earthquakes) also need targets. The speaker’s answer: the life-safety criterion sets the ethical floor only; within that boundary all other consequences enter the analysis with a much finer representation, following the decision maker’s utility.

Colin Caprani asked whether the questions posed for Europe could be abstracted to global principles – they are global questions in European language – and whether a unified treatment across domains is conceivable. The speaker agreed on the principles, but doubted that one value could serve all domains given the domain-specific consequence and cost models; the sensitivity of the result to the context of the criterion could be examined. Mahesh Pandey added from his experience with the Canadian nuclear industry that industries are reluctant to make life-safety valuations explicit, because an explicit number becomes a value judgment; implicit surrogates such as core damage frequency are preferred, which will be a challenge in every domain. A question from Emmanuel Ardillon (online) could not be taken because of a connection problem.
