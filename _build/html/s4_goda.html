Multi-Hazard Perspectives of Acceptable Shaking–Tsunami Risks for Coastal Communities#

Katsuichiro Goda, Canada Research Chair in Multi-hazard Risk Assessment, Department of Earth Sciences, Western University, London, ON, Canada

Session 4 · Thursday 22 October 2026, 11.43–12.10 (presentation and discussion)

Presentation slides (PDF) · original PowerPoint

Based on: K. Goda (2026), Multi-hazard shaking–tsunami fatality risk estimation for coastal communities, Earthquake Engineering & Structural Dynamics 55(11), 2618–2633, doi:10.1002/eqe.70220.

Summary#

The speaker, a reliability engineer by training who moved into geohazards and now works in an Earth Sciences department, presented a quantitative multi-hazard risk assessment for Tofino, British Columbia, as a practical case for the discussion of F–N curves and societal risk acceptance. The results, just published, had attracted public attention – with the media citing the very worst case (10−6 level, billion-dollar losses, hundreds of fatalities) – which illustrates both the interest in and the difficulty of communicating such results.

Setting. Tofino lies on the Esowista Peninsula on Vancouver Island, directly exposed to the Cascadia subduction zone; a full-rupture magnitude-9 event (the last in 1700) would mean a Sumatra- or Tohoku-type earthquake and tsunami for Canada and the US. The town centre on the tip of the peninsula is above 10 m; the beach areas with residences, campsites and resorts – about half of the population, the more expensive place to live – are below 10 m. About 2 500 people live in Tofino, and in summer more than 8 000 visitors come, so the exposure varies strongly over the year. Tofino is active in tsunami preparedness (evacuation maps, four sirens) and is also exposed to coastal flooding and sea-level rise (+0.7 m by 2100).

Model. Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability, integrated over a renewal-based stochastic catalogue. The occurrence model uses the 10 000-year turbidite history of Cascadia (about 40 events of M ≥ 8, 19 full ruptures reaching Vancouver Island, roughly one in 530 years), with radiocarbon dating uncertainty of ±100 years handled by resampling and a Gaussian-mixture renewal model; 5 000 stochastic rupture models (M 8.1–9.1) with heterogeneous slip, since slip distribution strongly affects the tsunami. Non-Cascadia (crustal and in-slab) sources contribute shaking only. The exposure is unusually good: an individual survey of the 1 789 buildings (value, occupants, material, seismic design), 2 001 populated sites with peak-summer daytime and night-time distributions, local site conditions (Vs30). Seismic fragility follows the Natural Resources Canada model; tsunami fragility is borrowed from the 2011 Tohoku data. Fatality rates differ fundamentally: even for the most severe shaking damage state they reach about 3 %, for tsunami inundation on average more than 15 %. Tides and sea-level rise are included, so that time-dependent risk at community level can be assessed.

Results. Multi-hazard exceedance-probability curves for financial loss are decomposed into non-Cascadia shaking, Cascadia shaking and Cascadia tsunami. Cascadia shaking dominates the financial risk because of the proximity to the rupture plane; the tsunami contributes about half as much over the whole community but about equally within the inundation zone. Scenario-based risk maps (shake map, inundation map, building damage and fatalities per location) support risk communication; in an extreme scenario shaking might kill about 14 people but the inundation about 200, where the water depth exceeds 10 m in the fully inundated beach area. The F–N curves for the single hazards and the combined risk show the tsunami dominating the fatality risk; for Tofino the multi-hazard F–N curve exceeds the local scrutiny line, falls in the upper range of the ALARP region and exceeds the tolerable risk threshold. Joint financial–fatality risk histograms show very different characteristics: shaking produces mainly financial loss (up to about 2 billion dollars), tsunami a smaller financial loss (half of the community is exposed) but a large fatality risk; combined, the behaviour is complex since both occur simultaneously in a cascading event.

Summary and outlook. A comprehensive multi-hazard risk model has been developed for Tofino; the financial and fatality risk curves for earthquakes and tsunamis differ substantially, and community-centred disaster risk reduction needs the joint curves. The fatality estimation can be enhanced by the dynamics of the exposed population, early warning and emergency response, and socio-economic and governance aspects. The speaker asked the audience how such quantitative results might be turned into policy and into a discussion about safety.

Discussion#

Summary of the discussion following the presentation (edited from the recording).

Jochen Köhler took up the F–N curves: their meaning depends on the system size, which is rarely stated – a country, a community or a single facility – and the system should be the one affected by the decision at hand. For Tofino the system is the isolated community under its mayor and emergency manager, with the province above it; regional results for the whole Cascadia coast exist as well, and Tofino was chosen because both shaking and tsunami matter there, with very different mitigation measures.

Arnold Yuan asked about the community’s response and about risk communication. Tofino is planning vertical evacuation structures and awaits funding; the speaker’s experience is that F–N curves are not understood even when shown, the public asking only whether the community is safe and what should be done. Experts therefore have to take responsibility and decide on the community’s behalf, but a mechanism to hear what those exposed want is needed – community asset mapping and “stress tests” of the community’s response capacity are the social-science methods used. Mahesh Pandey suggested expressing the additional mortality as a loss of life expectancy – a few days – rather than as an annual probability. Colin Caprani observed that the residents have implicitly accepted the risk by living there, so that the implicitly accepted level could be inferred and the evacuation structures reduce an already accepted risk; the losses in the billions reflect the high property values. Jochen Köhler added that the risk is partly voluntary, since people choose to live in such a beautiful place.
