## Summary

The presentation followed up on the discussion at the JCSS meeting in Aalborg (April 2026) on how reliability targets can be set from welfare optima rather than from a minimum acceptance criterion, and on what this implies for ISO 2394 and the Probabilistic Model Code.

**Two safety objectives.** Starting from the LQI $L = G^q E$, a baseline LQI without intervention and a post-intervention LQI are compared; spending on safety changes $G$, and the resulting change in mortality changes $E$. Two objectives can be formulated: *minimum acceptance* asks which requirements a design must satisfy (the marginal life-saving cost principle), *welfare maximisation* asks which acceptable design gives the greatest societal benefit. The two are nested: the optimum is sought among the acceptable decisions and is therefore always at or above the minimum. Graphically, with societal welfare (LQI) over the safety level, decisions to the left of the minimum should not be taken at all; to the right an optimum is reached.

**Derivation of the criteria.** The minimum criterion, written in the form used in ISO 2394 with the factor $K_1$, is obtained by setting the variation of the post-intervention LQI to zero at the boundary (marginal benefit equals marginal cost). The welfare-optimal criterion starts from the same point but carries additional terms; the main difference is a state-dependent mortality parameter, i.e. the optimum depends on the decision itself. Both criteria can be translated into reliability indices once a structural model (here the common lognormal resistance–demand model, staying "in the same lane as Rackwitz") is specified.

**Bridge example from ISO 2394.** Using the cost model and parameters of the ISO 2394 bridge example, the minimum and the optimum were plotted against the expected number of fatalities, together with the target reliabilities from the ISO table (economic optimisation). For this example the optimum is always above the minimum, but the two approach each other as the expected fatalities given failure increase. For small expected fatalities the minimum is not active and the usual target reliabilities can be treated as targets around which one may be above or below; for large expected fatalities the minimum becomes active and treating the target loosely becomes a problem. In many cases the monetary optimum used by Rackwitz and the welfare optimum derived here are practically identical.

**World map.** Using national data from the World Bank, the Penn World Table and the UN World Population Prospects (with "donor countries" for missing data, grouped along the regional classifications of integrated assessment models), the minimum and the welfare-optimal annual reliability index were computed for 168 countries and territories. The minimum varies by orders of magnitude in failure probability – very low in Central Africa, very high in Scandinavia – which is exactly the point of Bhattacharya's critique. The welfare optimum, in contrast, lies in a much narrower band for almost all countries. A parity plot shows the spread of the minimum from about 2.6 to above 4, while the optimum is concentrated. The absolute numbers are not the message; the spread is. Moving to the welfare optimum thus addresses much of the critique of the minimum criterion.

**Recommended safety format and conclusions.** Structural safety objectives should have a strict minimum – defined by balancing the marginal life-saving benefit against the cost of safety – and an optimum, defined by maximising the LQI within the acceptable set of decisions; the minimum may become active for large consequences. Deviations from the recommended format are possible but have to be justified – by robustness, model incompleteness, policy choices – not at will. The results are tentative and conditional on the models and assumptions; they expose the influence of the socio-economic and demographic parameters and should be adapted to the local context of each country rather than read off a world map. Feedback was invited to improve the manuscript before submission.

## Discussion

*Summary of the discussion following the presentation (edited from the recording).*

A first exchange, during the presentation, concerned why the minimum criterion leaves the failure costs out while the optimum includes them. Raphaël Steenbergen noted that the literature (Fischer and others) does the same and that one could argue either way; Jochen Köhler gave the rationale: the value of a structure must not affect the life-safety acceptance criterion – a more valuable structure does not justify a higher fatality risk – whereas an optimum governed by the failure cost lies above the minimum in any case.

After the presentation the discussion focused on the relation between the two criteria. Jochen Köhler observed that the two equations describe two decision problems of the same decision maker and differ essentially only in the valuation of fatalities – compensation or value of a statistical life versus societal willingness to pay. Raphaël Steenbergen asked how the minimum can cut the optimum for large numbers of fatalities if the optimum is by definition the larger; the answer was that this depends on the growth of the two denominators, i.e. on which fatality-related costs are included in the indirect failure cost *H*, and that for a given cost model the optimum has so far always been above the minimum. Mahesh Pandey raised the sensitivity of the optimum and the choice of the cost ratios, and Jannie Nielsen the sensitivity to the parameter *q*; the speaker had kept Rackwitz's values and the ISO example values throughout, so that the differences on the world map stem from the socio-economic and demographic parameters only. The state-dependent mortality term, which distinguishes the welfare optimum from a marginal criterion with a fixed value of life, was discussed without being fully resolved.

Jatin Nathwani read the minimum as society's protection against events with very large mortality consequences – the 1953 flood in the Netherlands – with the optimum governing the allocation of resources in all other situations, and stressed the difference between the calculus of a government (the Thames Barrier) and that of a private company. Jochen Köhler added a third decision problem, the private optimisation on top of the societal floor, and argued that when society is the decision maker behind the codes it should aim for the optimum rather than the minimum, the acceptance limit being the moral ground for the single cases in which the two conflict. Nathwani considered the curve of minimum versus optimum a major contribution that puts Bhattacharya's critique into perspective.

On the data side, Colin Caprani suggested a Bayesian hierarchical model with data imputation for the missing and inconsistent national data, which lets countries learn from each other and smooths arbitrary jumps across borders, warning that part of the observed spread might be an artefact of the treatment of missing data; the speaker had so far used regional "donor" countries following the regional classification of integrated assessment models. The discussion closed with Nathwani's account of how the LQI came about in the 1990s – the combination of life expectancy and the GDP as a measure of command over resources, with the working-time fraction as the exponent – before Rackwitz formalised it.
