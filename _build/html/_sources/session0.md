## Summary

Mahesh Pandey welcomed the participants to the University of Waterloo and handed over to Jochen Köhler for the introduction. A welcome address on behalf of the University was given at the start of the second day (see [Session 3](./s3_koehler.md)).

Jochen Köhler opened the workshop by noting that both days were being recorded in order to produce proper minutes, and that it was a particular pleasure to hold a JCSS workshop on societal risk acceptance in Waterloo, where the main developments in this area had taken place and two of the main persons behind them were in the room.

The topic is not new to the JCSS. In the early 2000s there had been lively discussions – Ditlevsen, Rackwitz and others arguing about very small details of the theory – which, together with the work of Lind, Nathwani and Pandey, became a common basis and a common understanding. Recently Mahesh Pandey has come forward with new insights, and the workshop was partly about discussing these insights and whether the paradigm should be changed.

*How safe is safe enough?* Safety is implicitly connected to all activities in society and always comes at a price, on the risk side as well as on the mitigation side. We are always somewhere between investing in mitigation and carrying risk – when driving through a tunnel, sitting in a lecture hall or receiving medical care – but we very seldom consider explicitly whether we are at the sweet spot. Every code and every rule implicitly contains an answer to how much should be invested to reduce the risk to life, property and environment; the question is always in the room but very rarely answered explicitly. The purpose of the research and of the discussions is to make this explicit and to have tools for an explicit societal choice of the risk to be accepted.

The developments in the JCSS are consolidated in ISO 2394:2015, which remains a consistent framework for structural design in general, with three levels of treatment (risk-informed, reliability-based, semi-probabilistic). The part on risk acceptance has attracted criticism: a very fair paper in *Structural Safety* by Bhattacharya made the point that the pure connection of the acceptance criterion to societal wealth leads to ridiculously high risk levels for very poor societies. When criticising in this direction one should remember that the LQI/SWTP criterion is a lower limit, which society invests in for ethical reasons; choosing a lower risk level for other objectives is always a fair choice. In the Eurocodes, risk acceptance is not treated explicitly but implicitly through target reliabilities for consequence classes; their magnitudes relate better to an economic optimisation philosophy than to a risk acceptance philosophy.

The workshop therefore wanted to maintain an active scientific discussion on the LQI: whether to stay with the break-even criterion or to use the LQI as a net-benefit criterion, and how the derived measures (SWTP, ICAF, SCCR, J-value) are to be interpreted – and to relate this to regulatory practice, with presentations from different world regions on the second day.

**Trondheim 2023 – where we left off.** Three and a half years earlier, the JCSS and IABSE Commission 1 had met in Trondheim for a [workshop on risk acceptance criteria](https://jcss-pmc.github.io/2023_Risk_Acc_WS) directed at practitioners, with participants from the Scandinavian oil and gas industry, regulators and consultants. There was a lot of disagreement in the group work, but a common denominator emerged:

- Risk is the expected consequence given the available knowledge; the estimate is knowledge-based, not objective. Risk is not an absolute property of an activity or an asset but the result of an analysis, and partly reflects the properties of that analysis.
- Risk acceptance is a decision – we always accept risk, implicitly or explicitly. The marginal comparison of mitigation options defines the acceptable risk level (the marginal life-saving cost principle), not the other way round. It is very hard to imagine an absolute risk acceptance criterion.
- Relative comparison rather than absolute limits: comparing the effectiveness of mitigation options within the same modelling universe is much more robust against epistemic uncertainty than any absolute treatment of risk. Upper limits serve as a reality check.
- Life safety and financial risk are different decision problems and should be formulated differently.
- Risk aversion exists, but F–N criterion lines with a slope steeper than one are an inconsistent and rudimentary way to treat the indirect consequences of large events; the JCSS argues for weighing possible fatalities consistently across situations.
- A transparent concept that can be standardised is needed; ISO 2394 is a good attempt, now ten years old.
- To be avoided: oversimplified "check-box" analyses, risk matrices and traffic-light representations that do not meet the requirements of a consistent analysis. The Norwegian offshore industry remains keen to maintain its absolute limits; there the workshop did not reach full agreement.

**Objectives.** To revisit the foundations – VSL, LQI, SWTP – and the recent critique; to hear how risk acceptance is regulated in practice in Europe, the Netherlands, Australia and North America; to foster a shared understanding of how societal preferences can rationally inform acceptance criteria and target reliabilities, and to decide whether the paradigm should be updated; and to identify key challenges, open questions and directions to be followed up in future meetings and task groups. The intended outcome is a short JCSS note on societal risk acceptance, as input to the Probabilistic Model Code and the ISO 2394 revision, and a documentation such that "we can remember in a few years what we have discussed".
