# Risk Acceptance Practice and Regulation in Australia – Australian Practice and the Case for Symmetry

**Colin C. Caprani, Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering, Monash University, Melbourne, Australia**

Session 3 · Thursday 22 October 2026, 10.35–11.11 (presentation and discussion)

[Presentation slides (PDF)](slides/Caprani_Risk_Acceptance_Australia.pdf)

## Summary

Before the talk, the speaker invited the community to ICOSSAR 2027 in Melbourne (December 2027 – the beginning of the Australian summer) and announced the release of PySTRA version 2, which now includes decision optimisation and reproduces some of the JCSS documentation and tutorials, as a low-friction way to get started with the numerics discussed at the workshop. The talk had two halves: what goes on in Australia, and the speaker's own take with suggestions for what the JCSS could do.

**Australia: prescribed numbers, implicit safety?** Australia has a formal reliability basis, AS 5104, which repackages ISO 2394 – but ISO 2394 presupposes a partial factor format whereas Australian standards are LRFD, following North American practice. The codes moved to limit-state format in the early 1980s with an attempt at calibration that has not been revisited since, so that people working with the standards keep running into the question of what safety is actually being achieved – a challenge the speaker has worked on for about ten years.

**Language matters.** Pulling apart what the standards achieve ultimately comes down to words. A terminological audit of 25 Australian standards (Caprani, Rizqiansyah and Zhou, *Language matters*, accepted in the Australian Journal of Structural Engineering) covering nearly 12 000 usages of the load-bearing words of structural safety – nominal, characteristic, design – flagged about 1 500 usages, a higher error rate than the Eurocode or the Canadian standard. A proposed vocabulary reserves *characteristic* for a nominal value with a stated quantile (the speaker disagrees with ISO 2394 allowing it to mean any nominal) and *design* for the verification value obtained through the calibrated factoring framework; Australian documents mix nominal and design values. A striking example: AS 5100.1 prescribes an ultimate design action with 5 % exceedance over the design life – which specifies a load quantile, not a probability of failure, and constrains the FORM design point to one slice through the failure surface; the design point is a joint calculation of β and the sensitivity factors.

**How much safety is implicit?** With a capacity reduction factor φ = 0.80 in bending, standard Super-T bridge girders achieve annual reliability indices near 7 (Melhem, Caprani and Stewart 2022); a proposed update to 0.85 was not accepted by the code committee "because it makes it less safe" – which it does, while remaining enormously safe. For a specialised code for the standardised U-trough rail bridge girders of the Melbourne broad-gauge network (Caprani, Khan and Ngan 2024), the baseline gives annual β of 6.2–6.6; reducing the live-load factor to 1.3 – about as low as the profession could accept for a well-specified rail line – still gives 5.5, and revising the resistance factors as well remains well above the selected targets of 4.4 (bending) and 4.7 (shear). Mark Stewart's calibration of the masonry standard raised φ for concentric compression from 0.45 – "the old factor of ignorance" – to 0.75.

**Prescriptions decide the bridge.** The UK FLOW footbridge (a standardised GFRP pedestrian bridge over rail lines) cannot be used in Australia: Network Rail allows supports 4.5 m from the running edge of the nearest rail for lines up to 125 mph, AS 5100 requires 10 m from the track centreline, with further provisions to 20 m and enormous collision protection otherwise. The 10 m is not a calculation; nobody can say where the risk acceptance behind it is. A copy-and-paste from AASHTO that dropped the 15° impact angle has made the 2 700 kN collision load applicable at any angle, and hundreds of millions of dollars have been spent on impact barriers around gantries near Melbourne's freeways. The really expensive decisions do not come from a detailed LQI calibration but from gross prescriptions that reflect a risk attitude in the mind of the profession; the risk acceptance in Australia is, in that sense, extremely low – "what if, what if, what if".

**Oddly in contrast,** the building industry has a quality problem: Opal Tower in Sydney (cracking shortly after opening in 2018, evacuation, declared defect-free only on 10 October 2026, eight years later), Mascot Towers, and the 1970 West Gate Bridge collapse that killed 35 workers and whose remnants stand in the Monash garden. The Shergold–Weir *Building Confidence* report (2018, 24 recommendations) concluded that it is not about β but about the competence and registration of practitioners, independent checking and traceability of responsibility from the design office to the site; quality assurance is exactly where the model world of reliability borders on the real issues. Designed, built and experienced reliability are three different things.

**The case for symmetry.** The marginal life-safety principle is already established; what is in the theory does not get out into the real world. The speaker framed the problem as a long-term trolley problem: a decision to make a structure extra safe commits society's resources and reduces protection elsewhere – the opportunity cost, mostly in public spending (nurses, police officers). Just because the people losing protection elsewhere cannot be seen does not mean the profession should ignore them. In code committees the conversation is "but you are making it less safe", followed by liability, reputational risk and "who will be blamed"; proposals are reviewed by senior gatekeepers whose technical expertise may be twenty years old and who do not know what β is, and who personally have no reason to take a risk a few years before retirement. Institutional defensibility (an approved process was followed) is not societal justification. Retaining a rule is as much a decision as revising it and should require the same evidence and the same current competence; at present the burden of proof is asymmetric. The speaker therefore proposed that *undue conservatism should be professionally unacceptable* – the levers are already in the codes of ethics of Engineers Australia, IStructE and ASCE (public welfare, current competence, reasoned scrutiny, resources and impacts) – and that the JCSS should state this ethical duty explicitly, require competent review of calibration proposals, and make clear that retaining an existing clause is not the default position: safety requires symmetry in moral attention, accountability and evidence. "The people we cannot identify are no less real."

## Discussion

*Summary of the discussion following the presentation (edited from the recording).*

The discussion centred on the opportunity-cost argument. Arnold Yuan, agreeing with much of the presentation, asked how the other side of the trolley problem – the protection lost elsewhere – could be quantified in an economic analysis. The speaker's answer was that it need not be modelled: in a democracy the allocation of released resources is a matter for the political process, which responds, imperfectly, to what the public wants; the profession's task is to define its minimum acceptable level and then neither undercut nor exceed it. Jochen Köhler qualified the symmetry argument: it assumes that the fraction of wealth society devotes to protection is constant, which is itself a societal decision – resources saved on structures need not go into other safety, and over-safe structures need not reduce other protection – so the argument should not become circular. Caprani accepted the qualification: the profession is responsible for its own domain, should use minimally what it demands through its standards, and must then trust the system to allocate the released resources.

Caprani added that the really expensive decisions in practice stem not from the calibration of β but from gross prescriptions that embody an uncalibrated risk attitude – the 2 700 kN collision load that lost its 15-degree angle when copied from AASHTO and led to hundreds of millions of dollars of impact barriers around Melbourne's motorways, or debates on crowd loading of handrails on rail bridges after a hypothetical carriage fire; Arnold Yuan noted how small the model world of reliability is compared with the decisions contained in a standard. Finally, Jannie Nielsen distinguished money from resources: economically it may be cheaper to make all walls of a building as thick as the most heavily loaded one, while differentiated thicknesses would be far more resource-efficient – so "economic optimisation" should be used with care. The speaker agreed that he uses money as a surrogate for resources.
