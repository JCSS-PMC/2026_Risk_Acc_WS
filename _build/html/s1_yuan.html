A Macroeconomic Framework for Determining a Proper Level of Investment on Infrastructure Renewal#

Arnold X.-X. Yuan, Department of Civil Engineering, Toronto Metropolitan University, Canada

Session 1 · Wednesday 21 October 2026, 13.36–14.26 (presentation and discussion)

Presentation slides (PDF)

Based on: R. Zhang, A. Yuan, C. Angyridis (2026), Beyond managerial efficiency: an economic welfare criterion for asset management planning, ASCE J. Infrastructure Systems (under review); R. Zhang, C. Angyridis, A. Yuan (2026), Potholes to prosperity: a micro–macro framework for growth-oriented road infrastructure investment allocation, Research in Transportation Economics 120, 101862, doi:10.1016/j.retrec.2026.101862.

Summary#

The presentation was offered as an “appetizer”: a non-structural problem – how much a city should invest in its road network – that leads to the same question as how safe is safe enough, namely which point on a cost–performance frontier society should choose.

The affordable level-of-service problem. The story started in Calgary in 2023. The city evaluates the condition of its roads every year in terms of a Pavement Quality Index (PQI); the backlog had reached CAD 550 million while the annual rehabilitation budget was CAD 40 million. The city council directed the administration to report back with a recommended PQI and the minimum annual capital budget needed to achieve it – in structural terms: what is the minimum safety target, and what is the minimum investment to achieve it? The analysis commissioned from the speaker’s group led, one year later, to a council decision to raise the budget to CAD 116 million.

What asset management can and cannot answer. Infrastructure asset management (IAM) follows a well-established workflow – inventory, segmentation, inspection, condition assessment, deterioration modelling, life-cycle strategies and network prioritisation. Network prioritisation is a combinatorial optimisation under budget constraints (here solved with deep reinforcement learning for tens of thousands of assets), which yields level-of-service (LOS) evolution curves for a given level of investment (LOI) and, in the steady state, a Pareto frontier of LOI versus LOS. The frontier is managerially efficient, but it does not say which point on it is the right one; that question lies outside the asset management exercise. Possible routes to an answer are public consultation, national benchmarking (e.g. the Canadian Core Public Infrastructure survey), risk-informed decision making (financial, safety, environmental and political risk) – or a macroeconomic analysis, which translates the Pareto curve into a societal welfare curve with an identifiable optimum between under- and over-investment.

The integrated IAM–DGE model. A neoclassical dynamic general equilibrium (DGE) model has three agents: firms produce GDP \(y_t = A\,k_t^{\alpha} L_t^{\beta}\) from private capital and labour; the government takes a share \(\tau\) through taxation; households split after-tax output between consumption and private investment, and lifetime welfare \(U = \sum_t \beta^t \ln c_t\) is the implicit criterion of the whole system, with the government acting as social planner. The innovation is to endogenise infrastructure: the production function is extended by public capital \(k_{g,t}\) and the quality of the infrastructure \(Q_t\) with an output elasticity \(\theta\); \(Q_t\) is exactly what the IAM programme delivers for a given investment \(i(Q)\). The government policy – the tax rate \(\tau\) – is then chosen to maximise welfare, expressed as consumption-equivalent variation (CEV), and the optimal \(\tau^*\) corresponds to an optimal level of service \(Q^*\). Calibration treats the current economy as a steady state; the policy change acts as a “shock”, after which the economy settles in a new steady state, much like a structural dynamics problem.

Calgary results. With \(\theta = 0.05\) the welfare curve is very flat around its maximum; with \(\theta = 0.01\) the optimum is sharper, at about CAD 106 million per year – close to the CAD 116 million proposed and adopted. The elasticity \(\theta\) is the key uncertain parameter: a meta-analysis of three decades of empirical studies (Bom and Ligthart 2014; about 780 estimates) gives an average of about 0.1 with a large spread; the first study (Aschauer, 1989) for the US interstate system found about 0.1, but municipal roads serve welfare more than economic growth, hence the lower value.

Extension to structural safety. The LQI can be derived from the lifetime utility of a consumption stream: with constant consumption, the utility \(c^q\) and the discounted remaining life expectancy at age \(a\), integrated over the age distribution, give \(L = G^{w/(1-w)} E\). The same ingredients appear in the DGE model. In a safety version of the framework, the investment on the left-hand side becomes a safety investment expressed through \(\beta\) or \(p_f\); the change in failure probability changes mortality, mortality changes labour \(L_t\) (and the working-time fraction), and the utility function is made LQI-compatible. The advantage would be that consumption no longer needs to be assumed constant but is produced by the dynamic equilibrium. The speaker stressed that this part is a proposal and a call for cooperation; everything else in the framework can be kept.

Conclusions. IAM planning gives a managerially efficient solution under a given budget but does not resolve the LOI–LOS trade-off; the integrated IAM–DGE framework offers a societal welfare perspective that identifies a unique LOI; the framework may be extended to structural safety target setting, with the LQI informing the formulation of the utility function.

Discussion#

Summary of the discussion following the presentation (edited from the recording).

The discussion turned first to the ethical question of connecting economic activity with investments in life safety. Jochen Köhler argued that the derivation of risk acceptance limits should keep the two apart: the marginal life-saving cost principle deliberately considers only the cost of the mitigation measures, so that an economic benefit can never become an argument for less risk mitigation. Arnold Yuan agreed that this was the question that worried him most. In his framework the benefit of a safety investment becomes visible through its economic effects, and the channel – as in the LQI – is the change in labour, the time available for leisure rather than work; what the framework does not yet consider is the distribution of the benefits within the population, an equity question. Jatin Nathwani added that the early LQI work had struggled with the same point and had concluded that safety and productivity reinforce each other: not investing in safety means more injury and illness, which reduces the productivity needed to generate wealth, so an either/or between safety and production is a false dichotomy.

A second thread concerned the key parameter of the model, the output elasticity of public capital θ. The empirical literature (about 780 estimates aggregated by Bom and Ligthart, 2014) scatters widely around small positive values, with Aschauer’s original 0.1 for the US interstate system as the starting point; for municipal roads, which serve welfare more than growth, the speaker had deliberately gone down to 1 %. θ strongly influences the optimal level of investment, and with 5 % the welfare curve shows no clear peak.

Several participants asked how the framework transfers to structural safety. A participant doubted that it can answer how safe is safe enough for the target reliability; the speaker explained that the link would be the investment-versus-β curve on the one hand and the LQI chain – β, mortality, labour – on the other, admitting that this is still abstract. Jochen Köhler pointed out that the relation between investment and safety level differs between types of infrastructure and between life-risk scenarios (collapse versus accident), so that the effectiveness of an investment in reducing the fatality rate would have to be filtered by activity; the speaker suggested the Ontario bridge inventory as a possible case study. Mahesh Pandey welcomed the framework as the more closed economic model that had always been intended to complement the steady-state LQI, and asked about the origin of the production-function exponents (A calibrated on the Calgary economy, α a default of about 0.3–0.35, labour constant). To the objection that the model is calibrated on an economy that already contains the intervention, the speaker explained that the current economy is treated as a steady state and a change in the policy variable τ acts as a shock after which the economy settles into a new steady state – the strength of the dynamic general equilibrium model.

Alex Cao closed by noting that the framework addresses optimality rather than acceptance, and Jochen Köhler framed this as the central question for the panel discussion: according to ISO 2394 acceptance considers only the cost of mitigation, never an economic benefit, while the speaker’s framework – as he confirmed – belongs to the optimality side.
