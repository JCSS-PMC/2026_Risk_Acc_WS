Reassessment and Reformulation of the LQI Framework for the Optimisation of Structural Safety#

Mahesh Pandey, UNENE Industrial Research Chair, Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering, University of Waterloo, Canada

Session 1 · Wednesday 21 October 2026, 15.31–16.57 (presentation and discussion)

Presentation slides (PDF)

Summary#

The presentation went back to the original thinking behind the Life Quality Index (LQI), traced how the derived measures and in particular the break-even criterion and the societal willingness to pay (SWTP) came about, and proposed a reformulation in which the LQI is used directly as the objective function of the structural safety optimisation.

Motivation. The LQI, conceived in the late 1990s, was connected to structural safety by Rackwitz and Ditlevsen, and the marginal life-saving cost principle was applied to real code calibration by Fischer, Faber and others; by then the framework seemed settled. The critique that the approach yields very different target reliabilities for different countries (Bhattacharya) – a paper the speaker reviewed and, like all reviewers, supported for publication – was the starting point for a reassessment: go back to what Lind wrote in the 1990s and see whether the original spirit of the framework can address the stated limitations.

Evolution of the LQI. Lind (1989) proposed a lifetime efficiency index: time of human life is the fundamental measure of value; the lifetime gained by a safety measure should exceed the working time consumed to pay for it. Quality-adjusted life expectancy (Lind, Nathwani and Siddall, 1991) was identified as the proper measure of human safety – lives cannot be saved, death can be postponed – but was hard to quantify. The UNDP Human Development Index (1992) inspired a surrogate with two dimensions, duration of life (life expectancy \(E\)) and quality of life (GDP per person \(G\) as the indicator with reliable data for almost all countries), balanced by the fraction of time spent working: the Life Product Indicator \(P = b^w e\) in the Waterloo “blue books” (motivated by Siddall’s question at the Atomic Energy Control Board whether the money spent on nuclear safety systems was diverting resources from other social programmes), renamed LQI in the 1997 “black book” (Nathwani, Lind and Pandey) and written \(L = g^w e^{1-w}\), then \(L = g^q e\). Two readings coexist in the literature: the LQI as a parametric function of algebraic quantities, and the LQI as a utility function – the expected lifetime utility of consumption of a statistically representative individual, with the lifetime \(T\) as the only random variable (Voortman et al. 2001, Pandey, Nathwani and Lind 2006).

The basic postulate of the framework from 1989 to 2000 was always maximise the total expected net benefit to society; the LQI serves to assess whether a prospect confers a positive net benefit compared with a base case, \(dL/L = w\,dg/g + (1-w)\,de/e > 0\).

How the break-even criterion and the SWTP came about. The first applications (Nathwani et al. 1997) assessed the efficiency of safety regulations from the only information available in the risk-analysis literature – lives saved and cost per life saved – so that a formal optimisation was impossible and the break-even line \(dL = 0\) served as a screening tool to separate clearly efficient from clearly inefficient regulations, with judgment for the marginal cases. The SWTP (Pandey and Nathwani 2003) arose in the context of the Darlington nuclear station: knowing only the cost of the safety systems, the implied gain in life expectancy that would keep society on the same LQI indifference curve was computed, \(\mathrm{SWTP} = (-dg)\,n = n\,(g/q)\,(de/e)\). SWTP is the marginal rate of substitution along the indifference curve, which necessarily differs between rich and poor countries (Canada: LQI 178, MRS 2 973 US\(; India: 103 and 496 US\)); the GDP dependence criticised later is built into the model rather than a new finding. SCCR, ICAF and the J-value are all derived from the same break-even condition.

Side effects. In retrospect the SWTP was a regressive development. It was derived for a special context but became generalised because it was simple to use: it turned into a minimum acceptable safety level, the original objective of maximising net benefit was forgotten (after 2006 there is no reference to LQI optimality), its proportionality to GDP caused disparities, and the frequently drawn conclusion that “economic optimisation leads to higher safety than the LQI” is simply a consequence of the SWTP not being an optimal result – which diminished the role of the LQI in setting safety standards.

Economic optimisation as the model. Optimisation as the basis for design codes goes back to Rosenblueth (1976) and was reformulated by Rackwitz from the mid-1990s: maximise \(Z(p) = B(p) - C(p) - D(p)\), which – since the benefit is independent of the design parameter – becomes a cost minimisation. Cost minimisation does not mean that the structure produces no benefit; the benefit is irrelevant to the choice of \(p\) given that the structure is built. Several formulations exist (first failure versus renewal process, finite versus infinite horizon, discounting, obsolescence) which give different optima; no normalisation per capita is needed.

LQI formulation of the same problem. With the same boundary conditions – a structure with annual \(p_f\) is put into service for a horizon \(t\) – the societal consequences are an economic impact (life-cycle cost distributed to the exposed population reduces GDP per person) and a life-safety impact (mortality increases by \(p_{d|f}\,p_f\), life expectancy decreases). The post-construction LQI \(L_2(p_f) = g_2^q e_2\) is always below the ideal \(L_0 = g_0^q e_0\) at \(p_f = 0\), since both impacts are losses: in this problem an LQI break-even condition does not exist, and using it as a constraint in cost minimisation is unwarranted. The objective is instead to minimise the reduction in LQI. Two computational issues deserve to be confronted rather than buried in derivations: how programme costs transfer to GDP (the common assumption distributes the total life-cycle cost equally over the exposed population and the horizon, like a tax; amortisation or insurance-premium logic are alternatives), while the change in life expectancy from a change in mortality is systematically covered by survival analysis.

Normalised form and bounds. Writing the life-cycle cost in cost ratios \(c_1, c_0, c_F\) normalised by the construction cost per square metre (Sýkora et al. 2017), the normalised LQI loss has a cost term proportional to \(C_{st}/g_0\) – the production time in years needed to cover the construction cost – and a life-safety term in expected life years lost, nearly linear in \(p_f\). If the cost is negligible, life safety governs and \(p_f \to 0\) for rich and poor countries alike; if fatalities are negligible, the problem reduces to cost minimisation; the inter-country differences are governed by \(C_{st}/g_0\) alone, which gives sensible and interpretable bounds for the target.

Canada and India. Construction costs from RSMeans (PPP adjusted; office building about 2 000 US\(/m² in Canada and 1 300 in India), GDP per person (48 600 versus 7 000 US\)), life expectancy (81.7 versus 70.2 years) and cost ratios inferred from the RSMeans cost breakdown (structure, roof and exterior envelope taken as safety related, about 24 % for an office building) were used. For expected fatalities of 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1 per m² of failed structure the target annual \(p_f\) for Canada and India comes out in the same order of magnitude – the ratio \(C_{st}/g_0\) is four times larger for India – and the LQI-optimal target for \(n_{d|f} = 0.01\) (\(p_f = 2.2\cdot10^{-5}\)) is close to the National Building Code of Canada target of \(\beta = 3\) for 50 years. The LQI target is lower than the cost-minimisation target and much more consistent across countries than the SWTP-based target, where the ratio of GDPs would give a failure probability about eight times higher for India.

The flood safety example (a reanalysis of the dike-height problem of Voortman 2003 and van Dantzig 1956) shows the “proper” formulation with a base case: an existing dike with \(p_{f0} = 10^{-4}\) per year (unacceptable) and the alternative of raising it. Here the base case has losses and the alternative has gains, so three criteria can be compared: LQI break-even (\(p_f = 3.4\cdot10^{-5}\)), cost minimisation (\(5.4\cdot10^{-6}\)) and maximum LQI gain (\(3.6\cdot10^{-6}\)).

Conclusions. The fundamental postulate of the LQI approach – manage risk to maximise the total expected net benefit to society – allows the LQI to be used directly as an objective function; the structural safety optimisation can be solved as the minimisation of a reduction-in-LQI functional, similar to cost minimisation; the break-even condition does not exist in that problem and its use as a constraint is unwarranted; the normalised form gives sensible bounds and more consistent targets across rich and poor nations, and thus overcomes the stated shortcomings of the LQI framework.

Discussion#

Summary of the discussion following the presentation (edited from the recording).

The reference state. The most debated point was the characterisation of the reference LQI L0 as the state with zero failure probability. Raphaël Steenbergen, Arnold Yuan and Colin Caprani questioned this abstraction: a structure with pf = 0 would cost infinitely much, structures are built to codes, and in the equation as written the construction industry reduces the GDP rather than contributing to it. The speaker explained that the formulation deliberately mirrors the life-cycle cost minimisation, which likewise rests on the decision to build already being taken, with the benefit cancelled; a more structured formulation with a base case – an existing unsafe structure and an improved alternative, as in the flood safety example shown at the end – yields gains and losses and thus a break-even point. Jochen Köhler agreed that the decision on the design parameter is conditional on the decision to build; the question whether current practice lies to the left or the right of the optimum – and whether the break-even should be referred to the status quo – remained open, with the tentative view that new structures are rather over-designed and existing structures with maintenance backlogs under-maintained.

Cost model and inputs. A participant new to the field asked why safety measures are treated as pure costs when they may also increase productivity, and where the loss of functionality enters; both can be accommodated in a more sophisticated model, the stylised linear formulation being a historical simplification. Jochen Köhler and Jannie Nielsen questioned the generalisability of the cost ratio C1: the safety-related share of the construction cost taken from the RSMeans breakdown (about a quarter for an office building) is a heuristic, the linear dependence on the central safety factor is not realistic, and only real structures designed to different consequence classes would reveal the actual cost of safety. The speaker acknowledged that these inputs drive the results and need sounder data if the approach is pursued.

Epistemic uncertainty. André Beck (online) raised the concern that the failure probabilities underlying all these criteria cannot be verified against observed collapse rates and may be wrong in either direction (neglected failure modes, hidden system safety). The speaker and Jochen Köhler answered that the analysis aims at orders of magnitude, that the optimisation uses the derivative with respect to the design parameter so that uncontrolled effects drop out, and that the seismic community’s detailed loss models show where a more refined treatment would lead. Arnold Yuan illustrated at the whiteboard the questions of reference point, status quo and the extra investment for extreme events.

GDP per person and solidarity. Colin Caprani objected that the GDP per person hides the distribution within a country – Mumbai versus rural India – and asked whether the Gini coefficient could correct this. The speaker replied that its range is too small to change the result much, but that the LQI optimisation in any case dampens the sensitivity to the GDP, whereas the SWTP-based target would make the failure probability eight times higher for India than for Canada. Jochen Köhler framed the issue as the choice of the community of solidarity – a nation, a region or the world – which is a societal decision. Arnold Yuan and Colin Caprani noted that differentiated targets within a jurisdiction are already practice, by uncertainty (construction quality) or by level of inspection, as in the Canadian bridge standard.

Convergence. Closing with the flood safety example, the speaker and Jochen Köhler found that they were “not on different pages”: the acceptance limit was always meant as a floor marking the domain of morally valid decisions, within which society should seek the optimum; the missing element in current code making is the articulation of a base case, and for discrete options in practice an optimisation is easier to handle than an effectiveness criterion.
