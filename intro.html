Societal Preferences on Risk Acceptance#

Workshop of the Joint Committee on Structural Safety#

University of Waterloo, Canada – 21 and 22 October 2026#

Context#

The workshop revisited an age-old question – how safe is safe enough? – and explored the societal, economic and engineering principles that form the basis for determining acceptable levels of risk.

Safety is fundamental and it comes at a price. Every code and every design decision contains an answer to the question, implicitly through target reliabilities and prescriptive rules. The answer is a societal one: how much should we invest to reduce the risk to life, property and environment? The rationale is rarely made explicit, and the rationales in use are not consistent across sectors and countries.

The JCSS has a long history with this topic. The discussions of the early 2000s, based on the work of Lind, Nathwani and Pandey on the Life Quality Index (LQI) and carried into structural safety by Rackwitz, Ditlevsen, Faber and others, led to the marginal life-saving cost principle and the societal willingness to pay (SWTP) as a criterion for life safety, consolidated in ISO 2394:2015 and the JCSS Probabilistic Model Code. More recent critique – the GDP proportionality of the SWTP-based criteria, and the question whether the LQI should be used as a break-even condition or as a measure of net benefit to be maximised – motivated this workshop. Holding it in Waterloo, where the LQI was conceived, was a deliberate choice.

The workshop also continued the JCSS/IABSE workshop on risk acceptance criteria held in Trondheim in June 2023, which had a focus on practice and regulation and closed with a set of commonly agreed principles. Waterloo picked up where Trondheim left off: the foundations of the criteria, their reassessment, and how risk acceptance is actually regulated in different world regions.

Goal and format#

The goal of the workshop was to foster a shared understanding of how societal preferences can rationally inform risk acceptance criteria for structures and infrastructure, and to identify key challenges, open questions and future directions for research, practice and standardisation.

The workshop consisted of invited lectures with ample time for discussion, a moderated discussion on the principles (Session 2) and an open discussion with conclusions (Session 5). Participation was in person and online. The workshop preceded the regular JCSS meeting of 22–23 October 2026 in Waterloo.

About this documentation#

These pages document the programme, each presentation (with the slides and a summary), the discussions after the presentations and in the two discussion sessions, and the conclusions drawn from them.

The discussions have been reconstructed from the recording of the workshop: the discussion after each presentation is summarised on the presentation’s page, the moderated discussion of Session 2 is documented in more detail along the topics of the panel. Contributors are named where they could be identified from the recording; otherwise a contribution is attributed to a participant. Online contributions are marked as such, and questions and comments from the chat are included where they were taken up. Corrections are welcome – please use the issue button at the top of the page or contact the editor.
